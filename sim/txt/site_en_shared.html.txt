# Forty-seven against two thousand six hundred
 The official enmity between Iran and Israel is forty-seven years
 old. Their relationship is about two thousand six hundred.
 This page is about the other 2,553 years — and about why that arithmetic matters to
 an Iranian today.

## First, the arithmetic
 Jewish presence in Iran goes back roughly 2,600 years.
 The official enmity between the two states began in 1979 — 47 years,
 which is under two per cent of the life of that relationship.
 | | Period | Length
 | Contact, from the Achaemenid era | ~2,600 years
 | Official enmity between the states | 47 years
 1.8%
 The enmity's share of the whole history.
 This is not a metaphor, it is a division:
 forty-seven divided by two thousand six hundred. That single figure says
 enmity is not a natural condition but a political decision —
 and decisions have start dates.
## And then, where we actually stand
### This page does not pretend the war away
 Since February 2026 a war has been under way in which Iran came under American and
 Israeli attack. A ceasefire was agreed in April 2026 and extended in June, but
 reporting says both sides have violated it repeatedly and the Strait of
 Hormuz has remained effectively closed. Cities have been damaged, people displaced,
 and executions have continued under the cover of the war.
 So, without decoration: this page does not say “we are really friends”.
 Today we are not. What it says is that the ordinary person in neither country chose
 this war, and that what existed before this enmity is larger than the enmity itself.
 If someone has lost their home, this page is not asking them to forgive anything.
## What is actually shared
 Five things, each with a source. Where we had no figure we wrote none —
 and in one place we rejected a well-known figure, because the primary source
 does not support it.
### The oldest thread
 Around 537 BC, Cyrus permitted the Jews exiled in Babylon to return and
 rebuild the Temple. In the Hebrew Bible, Cyrus is the only non-Jew called
 “messiah” — that is, anointed. The Cyrus Cylinder is in the British Museum
 today.
 A necessary caveat: the Cyrus Cylinder is not
 “the first charter of human rights.” That reading is largely a construction of the
 1950s onward; the text follows the standard form of Mesopotamian royal inscriptions,
 and the British Museum says as much. The thread is real; this particular title is not.
 The Book of Esther is set in the Achaemenid court at Susa, and the
 festival of Purim, still kept every year, comes from that account.
 The tomb attributed to Esther and Mordechai stands in Hamadan and was a
 Jewish pilgrimage site for centuries.
 Even in the language: Old Persian words survive in the Hebrew text of the Bible —
 among them pardes (which became “paradise”) and dat, meaning law.
 These are the loanwords of a long neighbouring, not a coincidence.
 And the strongest fact in this thread is the least often heard: the
 Babylonian Talmud — the foundational text of rabbinic Judaism after the Torah —
 was written on Sasanian soil. It carries Middle Persian loanwords and traces of
 Sasanian law. Judaism as practised today was, in part, shaped in Iran.
 Cyrus Cylinder: the British Museum. The texts of Esther and Ezra.
 For mythology and etymology, Encyclopaedia Iranica.
### A living community, not a memory
 The Jews of Iran are one of the oldest continuous Jewish communities in the
 world — roughly 2,600 years, from the Assyrian and Babylonian captivities.
 Before 1979, close to 100,000 Jews lived in Iran. Estimates today put it at
 8,500 to 9,100 — still the largest Jewish community in the Middle East
 outside Israel. One seat in the Majlis is reserved for a Jewish representative.
 And the other side of the same coin: 200,000 to 250,000 Israelis have Iranian
 ancestry, whole or partial. A significant number of Israelis have a grandparent
 who spoke Persian. These two populations are not strangers to each other; they
 are relatives.
 Population figures: the English Wikipedia entry “Iranian Jews” and the
 World Jewish Congress. These are ranges, not exact counts.
### A small chapter of the world war — and one correction
 Abdol Hossein Sardari, Iran's chargé d'affaires in occupied Paris, worked
 between 1940 and 1944 to keep Jews of Iranian origin in France outside the scope of
 Nazi anti-Jewish law. The United States Holocaust Memorial Museum calls him
 “an Iranian hero of the Holocaust”.
 But here we have to apply to one of our own favourite stories the same rule we
 apply everywhere else in this collection. It is widely repeated that Sardari issued
 “1,500 passports”. The Museum does not confirm that figure and calls it
 “implausibly high”. What is documented is a list of 91 people in May 1943 —
 and that most of that community survived the occupation.
 The smaller documented number is better than the larger undocumented one.
 What Sardari did for 91 people was still a large thing.
 United States Holocaust Memorial Museum, entry on Abdol Hossein
 Sardari — including its explicit rejection of the figure 1,500.
### The thirty years nobody remembers
 Iran voted against the partition of Palestine in 1947. But on 6 March 1950
 it recognised Israel de facto. What followed looks strange today:
 Oil sales from 1957; after the Suez Canal closed in 1967, the joint construction of
 the Eilat–Ashkelon pipeline; direct El Al flights to Tehran;
 cooperation in agriculture, medicine, water and road building; an Israeli school in
 Tehran; and exports that rose from $22.3 million in 1970 to
 $103.2 million in 1977.
 And then its ending, which has a precise date: six days after Khomeini's
 return, the Israeli mission compound was handed to Yasser Arafat and the street
 was renamed Palestine. The enmity has a birthday. Something with a date of
 birth can also have a date of death.
 Encyclopaedia Iranica, entry “ISRAEL i. Relations with Iran”.
### And the resemblances nobody counts on
 Both peoples are non-Arab in a largely Arab region; both have a
 pre-Islamic identity and a continuous literary tradition; both have enormous
 diasporas — and in Los Angeles those two diasporas are effectively one
 neighbourhood; and both societies carry a deep religious–secular fracture
 inside themselves, not against the outside.
 But the most important resemblance for this site is this: both populations have,
 in recent years, taken to the streets in enormous numbers against their own
 governments. Iran in 2022; Israel in 2023 against the judicial overhaul,
 week after week, in what reporting described as hundreds of thousands.
 One caveat is required: Israel's 2023 movement was a reform movement, not a
 revolutionary one — and Chenoweth herself stresses that her data covers maximalist
 campaigns, so the “3.5% rule” does not transfer to it. The comparison is invalid on
 that axis. But the simple conclusion stands: in both countries, the ordinary
 person's principal quarrel is with their own government, not with the people on
 the other side.
 For the 2023 protests: news reporting of the period (“hundreds of
 thousands”, and over 200,000 at a single event). For the methodological caveat:
 Erica Chenoweth. More in the Library.
## What this page does not say
 This section matters more than the one above it. Shared history can very
 easily be put to work justifying things that cannot be justified.
#### It does not say the war was right
 This page passes no judgement on the rights and wrongs of the 2026 war.
 Two thousand years of history justifies no bomb and returns no one to life.
#### It does not say change comes from outside
 The opposite. The whole argument of this collection — and the most explicit
 sentence Kadivar wrote about post-war Iran — is that the answer will not come from
 outside. No country has arrived at democracy by way of foreign attack, and the
 transition literature says this with data rather than slogans.
 Mohammad Ali Kadivar, MERIP, June 2026
#### It does not say Iran was always good to Jews
 “Almost uninterruptedly positive until 1979” is an overstatement. The Safavid and
 early Qajar periods include waves of forced conversion, segregated quarters and
 ritual-impurity law. The correct frame is “better than contemporary Europe,”
 not “always good.”
#### It does not resolve the question of Palestine
 Millions of Palestinians live under occupation and blockade, and no page about
 Iranian–Jewish kinship resolves that. Anyone who brings up shared history in
 order to push it aside is doing propaganda, not history. This page makes no such
 claim and refuses to be read in its place.
#### It does not say the Jews of Iran are free
 They live under the same government as everyone else. Reporting describes them
 walking a narrow line during the war: obliged to be Iranian and to stay clear of
 any accusation of ties to the enemy at the same time. The existence of that Majlis
 seat is not evidence of freedom.
#### And it asks nobody to forgive
 Historical kinship creates no obligation to forgive. Someone who has lost a person
 they loved has a right to their own anger. This page only says: send that anger to
 the right address — and the right address is a government, not a people.
## Why this matters for this collection
 The Europe talk carries a mandatory sentence that has to survive every revision:
 “This is about a state — not a religion, and not a community.”
 This page is that sentence in a mirror. If we want a European audience to separate
 the Islamic Republic from the people of Iran, then we have to separate the Israeli
 government from its people too.
 A rule applied only in one's own favour is not a rule.
 And practically it arrives at the same place as
 Your Card: an Iranian's step and an Israeli's step both
 point in the same direction — at their own government.
 What is your card?
